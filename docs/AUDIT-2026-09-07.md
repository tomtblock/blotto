# Repository audit — 2026-09-07

**Scope.** The whole repository as merged at `c0fa4a1`: the program, the five
services, the frontend, the tests, the scripts and every document. Commissioned
to find dead code, contradictions and anything published that the code does not
support, and to leave the documentation in one current shape.

**Method.** Every suite and gate run first, to have a clean baseline to work
against; then a dead-code sweep, a contradiction sweep across every constant and
claim, and an adversarial read of the money paths and the public surfaces.
Everything asserted below was checked against the code, not against a document.

**Headline.** The engineering is in good shape. All 767 tests were green before
this audit; 786 are green after it, the difference being the guards and the
security test this audit added. The money model is tight and the invariants that
matter are enforced by CI rather than by discipline.

Three things need your attention, in this order:

1. **The rules page publishes four operational commitments that no code keeps** —
   including a sanctions check. §1. This is the one that should change what you
   do next, and the decision is yours.
2. **One security defect in the program, found and fixed**: a stranger could open
   a tier's first pool and choose its sealing key, creating a pool that could
   take players' money and never pay or refund it. §2.1. The fix is in this
   branch and CI must confirm it, for a reason stated there.
3. **The documentation had drifted a long way behind the build** — the README
   said Phase 0, the handover described sixteen paid places, and the PID still
   describes a materially different competition. §4.

## 1. The one that matters

**We publish four promises that are not built.** The rules page and terms v2 say:

> "When a tournament closes, results are published and the tournament enters
> resolving. Resolving lasts up to 12 hours. **During resolving we check the
> winning wallet against published sanctions lists, as we are required to.** …
> **If no decision is made within 12 hours, the flag is cleared and the prize is
> paid.** … **You may appeal within 7 days** of being notified, and the appeal is
> decided by a different reviewer within a further 7 days."

None of the four bolded controls exists:

| Published | What is actually there |
|---|---|
| A `resolving` state lasting up to 12 hours | No such pool state, at the time of the audit. The states are Open, Sealed, Scored, Paid, Refunding, Closed. *Since built as a derived flag* (Stage C, same day): the read API computes it from held shares and the published window, and the app renders it; no program change |
| Sanctions screening of the winning wallet | Nothing, at the time of the audit. The word did not appear in any source file; the packet service's own header recorded that it shipped an always-clear answer, and its seam is at **entry**, not at resolving on the winner. *Since closed at entry* (Stages A–B, same day, see O10): every entrant is screened, and a listed wallet never holds a seat. The check on the **winner**: its on-chain half is built (Stage F — a sanctions hold both release paths refuse and `eligible()` excludes); the crank's screening call is the remaining change |
| A flag cleared and paid at 12 hours | No timer anywhere, at the time of the audit. The program has the seven-day permissionless release, which is published — correctly — as a safeguard rather than as the deadline. *Since built* (Stage D, same day): the daemon releases a still-held flagged share at `REVIEW_WINDOW_SECS` past the on-chain `escrowed_at`; the flag bit stays set by design, the escrow reaching zero is the record |
| An appeal route decided by a second reviewer | The review service records signed decisions. There was no appeal path. *Since built* (Stage E, same day): a player-signed lodge within seven days of an upheld flag, decided by a different registry member, all in the one signed log, with the form on the results page. The second reviewer is a founder-owned registry entry (O24) |

**How this happened, and it was mine.** You ratified the terms wording in the
addendum of 2026-09-07 and I shipped it in the copy pass of PR #67 in the same
sitting, ahead of the Phase 2 and Phase 3 work that builds what it describes.
The sequencing was wrong: copy that describes a control should not land before
the control does. `CONTRIBUTING.md` has a rule for exactly this — "a surface that
cannot prove the claim it makes must not make it" — and the copy pass went
straight past it.

**What I have done about it.** Three things, none of which closes the gap:

1. `01_PRODUCT_FACTS` gains a §9 listing all four, because that is the document

2. `app/src/copy/published-claims.test.ts` is a new register: every operational

   The four with no artefact are declared as gaps with the phase that closes

   made, if a proof file disappears, or if the gap set changes without the
   documents changing with it.
3. `docs/AS-BUILT.md` §6 states them plainly under "what is not built".

**What I have not done.** I have not touched the copy. It is your ratified

## 2. Bugs found and fixed

One security defect was found in the program and fixed. Nothing was found that
could move money to the wrong place; the defect could **strand** money, which is
the other way to lose it.

### 2.1 A stranger could open a tier's first pool — and choose its sealing key

**Severity: high. Fixed, and the fix is in this branch.**

`open_pool` is keeper-gated with a liveness fallback: if a tier has sat idle for
24 hours past its cooldown, any signer may open it, so a dead daemon cannot
freeze a tier forever. The window is measured from `Config.tier_next_open_at`.

That field was seeded to **zero** — by `init_config`, by `migrate_config`, and
for any tier the founder later enabled. Zero plus 24 hours is 2 January 1970, so
for a tier that had never settled a pool the fallback was *always* satisfied and
the gate was not a gate at all. The code comment said so and called it
"harmless: the gates below still decide".

It is not harmless, because of one detail elsewhere in the same instruction: **the
opener supplies `sealing_pubkey` and `sealing_secret_hash`.** A pool opened by a
stranger therefore carries a sealing key our custody does not hold, and:

- `reveal_pool_secret` can never land — it checks the secret against the hash the
  opener committed, and only the settlement signer may call it;
- `commit_results` is blocked behind the reveal, so `pay_prizes` never runs;
- and once the pool seals at `MIN_SEATS` or more, `trigger_refund` refuses it too
  (it requires state `Open` and a field below the minimum).

So a stranger could create a pool that looks entirely normal on our own site,
take 52 to 64 players' entries, and leave the pot and every entry with **no
instruction able to move them**. Cost to the attacker: the pool's rent, about
0.0068 SOL.

The devnet exposure today is nil — tier 0's slot is held by pool 1 and tiers 1
to 6 are disabled — but it would have opened the moment you enabled a new tier,
and an attacker watching for `TiersOpened` could have front-run the keeper.

**The fix**: `init_config` and `migrate_config` stamp every tier's
`tier_next_open_at` with the current time, and `set_open_tiers` restamps any
tier it takes from disabled to enabled (keeping a cooldown that is still
running), so the liveness clock starts when a tier becomes usable rather than at
the epoch or at a deployment months earlier. It costs the keeper nothing —
`open_pool` requires `now >= tier_next_open_at`, and `now >= now` holds — and it
puts a stranger a full fallback window away from the moment of enablement.
`open_pool` additionally refuses a non-keeper outright when the stamp is zero,
so a future path that forgets to stamp cannot reopen the hole. The first cut of
this fix stamped at enablement only when the field was zero; CI's LiteSVM lane
showed that leaves the deployment stamp in place for a tier enabled later, which
is the same hole delayed by one fallback window — corrected in this PR before
merge.

**How it hid.** A test in `gates.rs` asserted that "any funded signer opens pool
1" — correct under the permissionless D-A1 design, and never revisited when the
keeper role landed on 2026-09-07. It kept passing, which is precisely why nobody
noticed the gate was inert. That test now asserts the refusal, and there is a new
one covering a newly enabled tier end to end.

**⚠ Verification caveat, stated plainly.** This container has no Solana SBF
toolchain, so `anchor build` cannot run here and the compiled artifact in
`target/deploy/` predates the fix. The program's own unit tests, clippy and fmt
all pass locally. The LiteSVM suite cannot: run against the stale artifact, the
four tests that encode the new behaviour fail — and the way they fail is itself
the proof, because `gates.rs` reports that the stranger's `open_pool`
**succeeded**. **CI's `on-chain` lane must go green before this is believed**, and
if you would rather not carry a program change into the deploy window at all,
reverting the three source files in this commit is clean and I will say so
plainly: I would not deploy without it.

### 2.2 PRODUCT-FACTS told counsel the program enforces something it cannot

The allocation-invariant row read *"five u8 summing to exactly 100; the program
rejects anything else"*, citing `register.rs`. The program never sees an
allocation — `register` takes `sha256(battles‖salt)` and nothing else, and the
plaintext exists nowhere on-chain until the pool secret is published. That is the
sealing property; it is the whole design.

The same false claim appeared in `CLAUDE.md`'s hard rules and in the header of
`backend/scoring/src/allocation.ts`, which went further and said a divergence
from the on-chain check "is a defect in this file".

The invariant *is* enforced, in the two places it can be: the client at
submission, and the scoring engine at reveal. **I checked the failure path
before rewriting the claim**, because a malformed sealed set that made the engine
throw would have halted settlement for a whole pool — one entrant able to freeze
sixty-three other people's money. It does not: `crank.ts` catches the decode
failure and scores that entry all-losses by the same published rule as a lost
ciphertext, and the bundle publishes its status. The defence was already there;
only the description was wrong.

Fixed in all three places. The corrected statement is stronger for being true.

### 2.3 `vault.rs` described the wrong exit set

The module doc on the file that *is* the money chokepoint still listed the exits
as they stood before your ruling of 2026-09-07: it named `forfeit_escrow`, which
now moves no lamports at all, and omitted `pay_next_place`, which is the real
fourth exit. `CLAUDE.md`, `PRODUCT-FACTS` and the CI check were all correct; the
file itself was not. Someone auditing the money model by reading the chokepoint —
which is the sensible way to audit it — would have got the wrong answer.

### 2.4 A safety check written and never wired

`app/src/chain/rpc.ts` exists for one rule: a preview deployment must never point
at mainnet. It exports `looksLikeMainnet` and says in its own comment that it
exists "so a build can assert on it rather than discover the problem from a
user's transaction". Nothing asserted on it. It does now, in every CI lane and
every Vercel preview build.

### 2.5 The TypeScript mirror of the program's constants was never checked

`prizes.ts` calls itself a mirror of `constants.rs` for ten values — the minimum
field, the fill deadline, the cooldown, the pots, the prices. Nothing compared
the two. The vector suite pins the entry prices against the Python oracle and the
LiteSVM suite reads the Rust side, but no test crossed the language boundary, so
a change to one file and not the other would have shipped: both sides would have
stayed internally consistent and every suite would have stayed green. There is
now a test that reads `constants.rs` and compares.

### 2.6 The rehearsal budget was arithmetic from a product that no longer exists

Stage D of the devnet runbook — the twenty-pool rehearsal — was costed at a
5.12 SOL pot swept back from sixteen winner wallets, with a treasury gain of
1.28 SOL per pool. Since D-E1 and D-E2 the pot is 4 SOL and there is one winner.
The pool-1 pass/fail gate, which is the thing that catches a silent sweep failure
before the bank empties, was checking for the wrong number.

Corrected where the arithmetic is exact: the gate is now "about −0.41, not about
−4.41", the treasury gain is +2.4 SOL per full pool, and the floor before pool 1
can open is 4.5 SOL. **Deliberately not corrected**: the twenty-pool
working-capital table and the sweep cadence derived from it. Both fold rent, fees
and cycle timing into a model nobody wrote down, and inventing replacement
numbers would have been worse than flagging them. They are marked for
recomputation from a measured pool 1, which is what pool 1 was always for.

### 2.7 A truncating cast in the treasury floor

`idle_tier_reserve` accumulates the policy reserve in `u128` — correctly, since
it is a product that can grow — and then returned it with a plain `as u64`.
CLAUDE.md requires the `u128` intermediate and this had it; what it did not have
was a decision about the way back down. A wrapping cast on a FLOOR understates
it, and an understated floor releases money the policy exists to hold.

The sum is unreachable at any configured tier — seven tiers of 300 SOL at the
maximum reserve is a rounding error against `u64::MAX` — so this was never a live
bug. It now saturates instead, so the direction of failure is chosen rather than
left to a cast: overstating a floor blocks a withdrawal, which is the safe way
round.

## 5. Amber warnings

Not bugs. Things that are one bad day away from being one.

**A1 — CLOSED 2026-09-07 (see O8): the deploy now stages all three knobs for
all three services; it remains to confirm them from outside after the next
deploy.** As found: the
shared hygiene module has three knobs and all three default to off, which was
correct when nothing was deployed. The indexer's read API, the packet service and
the review service are now on Fly behind public hostnames, and none of the flags
appears in any committed config. If they are on, they are on as deploy-time
secrets. `fly secrets list` is how you find out, and turning them on for each
public service is on the checklist.

**A2 — The settlement key lives in two processes, one of which is a public HTTP
service.** The review service and the daemon read the same
`SETTLEMENT_SIGNER_KEYPAIR`, decoded into tmpfs at boot. It cannot withdraw
anything and it cannot forfeit, but see §6 for what it can do. Turnkey
co-signing (E6) is deferred and this is the reason to un-defer it.

**A3 — The Turnkey export policy has never been asserted against the real API.**
The sealing property's actual enforcement is Turnkey refusing to export a pool
secret before its pool seals. Everything in CI tests `LocalCustody`, which
enforces the same semantics in-process. The real assertion is a Phase 5
rehearsal step and it has not been run. The most load-bearing verifiability claim
in the product currently rests on an interface, not on a tested policy.

**A4 — CORRECTED 2026-09-07. This finding was wrong.** It said the app writes
battle stills to the player's browser and never reads them, and recommended
deleting the write path. The stills ARE read: `allStills()` feeds the **My
Blots** grid on the profile page (`app/src/app/profile/my-blots.tsx`, mounted
by `profile-client.tsx`), a shipping feature. I had checked one reader,
`stillsForPool`, and generalised from it.

What was true is much smaller: `stillsForPool` alone had no caller, and it is
now deleted. Nothing else changes, and deleting the write path — which this
finding recommended, and which was briefly ratified on the strength of it —
would have removed a working feature.

**A5 — A dust-sized forfeited share splits unevenly.** If a forfeited share were
ever smaller than the number of eligible players, the equal split breaks down:
the remainder-taker must be paid first or the others' portions round to zero and
the call is refused. All the money still reaches players and nothing is stranded,
but the division would not be equal. At a 4 SOL pot this is unreachable; it would
need a pot in single-digit lamports. Recorded rather than fixed, because the fix
adds a branch to the most safety-critical handler in the program for a case that
cannot occur at any configured tier.

**A5b — A pool whose sealing key is lost can never pay and can never refund.**
This is the design risk behind §2.1, and it outlives the fix. `trigger_refund`
requires state `Open`; once a pool seals, its only route out is
reveal → commit → pay. If the secret becomes unavailable for any reason — a
Turnkey outage that ends in a deleted key, a custody migration gone wrong, or a
pool opened by someone else — the pot and 52 to 64 entries have **no on-chain
route to anyone**. The operator could make players whole from the treasury, but
the program could not.

Two ways to close it, both design decisions rather than patches, which is why I
have not built either overnight:

- **A stuck-pool refund.** Let `trigger_refund` also accept a `Sealed` pool past
  its deadline by a wide margin with no secret revealed. The complication is
  real: sealing releases that pool's entries from `platform_refundable`, so the
  treasury may legitimately have spent them by then. Closing it properly means
  keeping entries refundable until results are committed, not until seal.
- **Remove the liveness fallback.** It exists so a dead keeper cannot freeze a
  tier, but a frozen tier strands nothing — no pool means no money at risk — and
  the admin can rotate the keeper at any time. Meanwhile a pool opened by a
  stranger is strictly worse than no pool. Sealing and refunding an *existing*
  pool are already permissionless, and those are the properties that actually
  protect players. **My recommendation is to remove it**, which makes §2.1's
  whole class of problem unreachable.

**A6 — The pot derivation is in two source comments.** `constants.rs` and
`prizes.ts` both state how the pots were arrived at. Addendum ruling 6 keeps that
off every public surface, and it is out of every document. Source comments in an
Apache-2.0 repository are a public surface the day the repository is public.
**Your call** whether that day is coming; if it is, the two comments should go
the way the documents went.

**A7 — `migrate_config` accepts any keeper, including the zero key.** Setting it
to a key nobody holds would freeze new pools in every tier until the 24-hour
liveness fallback lets anyone open one. Self-healing within a day, and fixable
with `set_keeper`, but there is no guard against the typo.

## 6. Attack vectors

Ranked by what they would actually cost us. Each names its current mitigation
honestly, including where the mitigation is detection rather than prevention.

### V1 — Settlement-signer compromise redirects a pot (high impact, detectable, not preventable)

The settlement signer commits the results. The program validates that the payout
list is seat-sorted, duplicate-free, within the field, and sums to exactly the
pot — but **not that it reflects the actual scores**. An attacker holding that key
could commit a list paying the whole pot to a colluding entrant, then pay it.

Every payment goes to a wallet bound to an `Entry` account, so the key cannot pay
itself, and the four service keys are refused entry. The published bundle and the
verify page make the fraud provable afterwards by anyone who recomputes — which
is exactly the trust structure the product advertises. But the money has moved by
then.

*Mitigation available now:* Turnkey co-signing for `commit_results` (E6). That is
the single highest-value security change on the list, and A2 compounds it.

### V2 — Under-presentation in a next-rank split (medium impact, disclosed)

When a forfeited share passes to the next competition rank, the program verifies
each recipient's rank by Merkle proof but cannot verify that the presented group
is *complete*. A settlement signer presenting three of five eligible players
inflates those three portions.

This is a known, deliberate residual: the group's completeness is the signer's
assertion, it is disclosed on the rules page under your addendum ruling 4, and
the published bundle names the whole group so any deviation is visible. At first
place — the common case — there is no residual at all: the committed payout list
*is* the group, and the program computes the size, the eligible set and the
remainder recipient itself and refuses any call that disagrees.

### V3 — Pool-secret exposure before a pool closes (catastrophic, structurally defended, one untested link)

Anyone holding a pool's X25519 secret before sealing can read every entry and
enter a set that beats them all. Defence: the secret is generated in-process,
imported to Turnkey, wiped locally, and export-locked by a policy that reads
chain state rather than a local flag; the read API refuses ciphertexts before the
reveal; the daemon does nothing at all when custody and the chain disagree.

Two honest caveats. The operator generates the key, so this is a policy
guarantee, not a mathematical one — which the rules copy now states plainly. And
see A3: the Turnkey half has never been exercised.

### V4 — Public read API as a denial-of-service lever (medium, unmitigated)

Unrated JSON endpoints in front of Postgres, several of which do multi-table
joins per request. See A1. Nothing here touches money; it takes the site down and
takes the daemon's RPC budget with it.

### V5 — Keeper griefing (low, self-healing)

The keeper decides only *when* the next pool opens within the rules, not whether
a pool runs or who wins. A dead or hostile keeper stalls a tier for at most 24
hours past its cooldown, after which anyone can open. Sealing and refunding were
already permissionless. This is a well-designed piece of the system.

### V6 — Malformed sealed entry as a settlement halt (closed)

Checked explicitly, because it would be severe: one entrant sealing bytes that
break the allocation invariant, freezing everyone else's money at reveal. The
crank catches the decode failure, scores that entry all-losses by the published
rule, and continues. Already defended before this audit; recorded so nobody
removes the `try/catch` thinking it is defensive clutter.

### V7 — Admin-key compromise (high impact, accepted, structurally bounded)

The admin can pause entries, change the tier tables for future pools, forfeit a
flagged share, and withdraw from the treasury above the policy floor. It cannot
reach a prize vault: a forfeited share stays in its pool and can only be divided
among players. So the worst case is the operator's own working capital and a
denial of service — never a player's prize. That bound is the product of the
no-withdraw rule and is worth defending as hard as it has been.

*Mitigation pending:* admin and upgrade authority are a single devnet fee payer
today. Squads before mainnet, which is on the checklist and was always the plan.

## 7. Best practice — what to keep doing, and four things to add

**Keep.** Three habits in this repository are doing real work and are unusual
enough to name: invariants enforced by CI greps rather than by review
(`check-standing-rules.sh` is the reason the vault's exit set is a fact rather
than an intention); a Python oracle generating the scoring fixtures, so a vector
captured from our own output can never prove itself; and comments that explain
*why* rather than *what*, several of which are the only surviving record of a
decision. The audit was possible in a night because of the third one.

**Add, in the order I would do them:**

1. **A claims register for every surface, not just the rules page.** The one
   added here covers the rules copy. The landing page, the pool page and the
   share card all make statements too.
2. **Cross-language pins wherever a value is mirrored.** The constants mirror
   now has one. The event layouts have one. The error-code list in
   `app/src/tx/errors.ts` mirrors `error.rs` *by position* and has none — the
   failure mode is a user shown the wrong reason their transaction failed.
3. **A "deployed configuration" check in CI.** `check-deployed.ts` verifies the
   program's wire contract. Nothing verifies that the services' security knobs
   are on, and A1 is what that costs.
4. **Date the phase reports in their titles.** They are history, and they read as
   current. `INDEX.md` now says so, but a reader who opens one directly does not
   see `INDEX.md`.
